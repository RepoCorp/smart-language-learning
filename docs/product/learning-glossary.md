# Learning glossary

Shared vocabulary for discussing the learning experience. This glossary defines
concepts, not how they are implemented or how they should be named in the interface.

## What we learn

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Item** | A unit of learning: something the student wants to understand, remember, or use. A word, phrase, or pattern can be an item. |
| **Word** | A vocabulary unit with a meaning and a use. Learning it involves more than recognizing its translation: it also means becoming able to use it. |
| **Expression** | A group of words understood or used together as a unit. Its meaning is not always obvious from the individual words. |
| **Phrase** | A piece of language learned as a whole. In our product vocabulary, this can mean a short expression, a sentence, or several connected sentences, not only a grammatical phrase. |
| **Pattern** | A recurring structure or relationship that helps the student understand or produce more than one particular word or phrase. Learning a pattern means noticing what different examples have in common. |
| **Learning bank** | The collection of items the student has chosen to learn, whether they have started practising them or not. Also referred to as the study list or learning deck. |
| **Example** | A concrete instance that illustrates a word, meaning, or pattern. The example and the pattern it illustrates are different things to learn. |
| **Context** | The surrounding language or situation that helps explain what something means and how it is used. |

An **item type** describes what is being learned, such as a word, phrase, or
pattern. A **word type** describes the role of a word, such as noun, verb, or
adjective. These are different classifications.

## Groups of patterns

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Word pattern** | A recurring structure within words or a relationship between word forms. The focus is on the word rather than the organization of a sentence. |
| **Word-building pattern** | A way existing words are formed from other words or meaningful parts, creating related meanings. It is a kind of word pattern, not permission to freely attach a piece to any word. |
| **Affix pattern** | A word-building pattern involving a meaningful piece attached to a word or its base. “Affix” is useful for our discussions; “word building” is usually more approachable for learners. |
| **Prefix pattern** | An affix pattern where the recurring piece appears at the beginning. |
| **Suffix pattern** | An affix pattern where the recurring piece appears at the end. |
| **Phrase pattern** | A recurring way words or word forms work together across a phrase or sentence. It can involve ordering, relationships between words, or a structure expressing a particular meaning. |
| **Construction pattern** | A reusable combination or arrangement of language that serves a particular meaning or function. We use this term for phrase-level structures, rather than for the individual words appearing in them. |
| **Pattern family** | A group of patterns that share a common principle, such as building words with endings. A family is not a language: similar kinds of patterns can exist in different languages. |

These groups describe different scopes and can overlap. Learning a particular
verb as vocabulary is different from learning a recurring way verbs behave in
sentences. Likewise, learning a word containing an ending is different from
learning the relationship expressed by that ending.

**Grammar feature** means a property or structure we can notice in an example.
**Grammar rule** is an explanation of how that feature or pattern works. A feature
describes what is present; a rule explains it; a pattern is the recurring
relationship the learner is trying to recognize and use. These can describe the
same underlying idea from different perspectives.

## How we learn

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Strategy** | An approach or resource that helps the student understand, associate, explore, or remember something. Looking at examples, comparing forms, or making a memorable association are strategies. |
| **Exercise** | An activity for practising and strengthening a skill. It can provide hints, repetition, or other support. Its main purpose is learning through practice. |
| **Evaluation** | An activity for checking what the student can understand, recall, or produce. Its main purpose is assessing learning, including through the student's own judgment. |
| **Self-assessment** | An evaluation in which the student compares their response with the answer and judges whether they knew it. It need not involve typing or automatic grading. |
| **Initial presentation** | The student's first guided encounter with an item: an opportunity to understand and become familiar with it before being expected to remember it. |
| **Review** | Returning to previously introduced content to recall it and reinforce memory. In spaced repetition, reviews are scheduled rather than repeating everything equally often. |
| **Session** | A period devoted to learning. It can combine discovering items, exploring them, practising, and evaluating recall. A session is not just a series of tests. |
| **Item view** | The place for looking closely at one item and exploring ways to learn it. It is not limited to the first time the student sees that item. |

The key distinction is purpose: a **strategy helps learning**, an **exercise
practises**, and an **evaluation checks**. An activity can support more than one
purpose, but these concepts should not be treated as synonyms.

## Directions and memory

- **Recognition:** understanding language when encountering it. The student sees
  or hears a word or phrase and works out its meaning.
- **Production:** finding or constructing the language needed to express a
  meaning. Understanding something does not necessarily mean being able to use it.
- **Learning direction:** which side the student starts from, language or meaning.
  Recognition and production are complementary abilities and may develop at
  different speeds.
- **Spaced repetition:** returning to material at intervals to strengthen long-term
  memory, instead of practising everything every day with equal frequency.
- **Recall:** bringing something to mind, rather than merely recognizing it after
  the answer has been shown.
- **Difficult-item practice:** additional practice for material that needs more
  support. It is not the same thing as evaluating it again.

## Sources of learning material

- **Curated examples:** deliberately chosen examples that illustrate something
  clearly. They need not come from the student's personal content.
- **Bank examples:** examples drawn from items in the student's own learning bank.
  They connect a general pattern with material the student has chosen to learn.
- **Generated examples:** examples created on request rather than selected from
  existing material. Generation describes their origin, not their learning purpose.
- **Dialog:** a conversation used as learning material, providing connected
  language in a meaningful situation.
- **Conversation practice:** participating in an exchange to practise understanding
  and expressing meaning, rather than only studying an existing dialog.
